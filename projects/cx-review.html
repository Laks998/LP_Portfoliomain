CLEARISK · CX PORTAL — ONBOARDING REVIEW · B2B SAAS

Improving productivity on a live onboarding review workflow (B2B SaaS)

I co-built CX Portal's design system as one of two designers on the product. Months after launch, I sat in on two live reviewer sessions to see how the screen actually held up under daily use — and watched a handful of small habits quietly cost the team time on every single application. This is the story of what I found, and what I changed.

My contribution Design system (co-creator) · Workflow observation · Flow diagnosis · Interaction design · UI design
Project overview

A trade-processing platform built to replace a patchwork of tools

CleaRisk is a derivatives trading platform offering trade processing and client lifecycle management for financial institutions. The team had been running onboarding, back-office work, documents, and agreements across several disconnected tools, so they set out to build one piece of software — CX Portal — to hold client lifecycle management end to end.

I joined as one of two UX/UI designers on CX Portal, and spent my first stretch on the product co-building its design system alongside every new screen we shipped: colors, type, a 12-column grid, and a component library covering navigation, buttons, tables, search, and icons. Once the core product was live, my role shifted — from designing new screens to watching how the ones already shipped held up under real, daily use. This case study is about the morning that shift mattered most.

01 — Two mornings with the review queue

I watched two reviewers work through real applications, start to finish

The review screen wasn't broken in any way a heuristic checklist would have caught — nothing crashed, nothing was mislabeled, nothing failed an accessibility scan. So instead of running an audit, I asked to sit in on a screenshare with two of the compliance reviewers who worked this queue every day, and watched them move through a stack of real applications from start to finish. I ran the same watch-and-log pass twice, a week apart, with a different reviewer each time, so I wasn't reading too much into one person's habits, or one unusually messy application.

"Wait — why was this rejected?"

Twenty minutes into the first session, a reviewer picked up an application her colleague had rejected that same morning. She read through it once, then turned to me and asked the question that ended up shaping most of this case study.

"Wait — why was this rejected?"

There was nothing attached to the Reject button to answer her. Applications get reassigned between reviewers constantly, and without a logged reason, that question had nowhere to go except back to whoever made the original call — if they were even still online to ask. I'd already co-built the Timeline component on this exact screen, originally just to log system events: submissions, edits, third-party checks passing or failing. Rather than invent something new, I extended it. Approve and Reject now require a comment before either will fire, and that comment posts to the Timeline with a timestamp and the reviewer's name, right alongside the system events already logged there. The next person to open the case sees the reasoning before anything else on the screen — no Slack thread to dig through, no waiting on whoever made the original call to log back on.

"How do I edit any of these details in case of a mistake?"

An hour later, in the same session, she caught a typo in a field she'd already approved and went looking for a way to fix it. There wasn't one — once a section was approved, the button just read "Approved," the client-facing state locked in, and Reject disabled right alongside it.

"How do I edit any of these details in case of a mistake?"

She flagged it to a manager instead — exactly the kind of workaround that shouldn't need to exist for something this small. The fix extended the same Edit control already used everywhere else on the form: clicking it unlocks the fields and logs the change to the Timeline like any other edit. The part that actually mattered was what happens on save — the section drops back to Pending instead of staying marked Approved, so a quiet correction doesn't quietly break the thing the approval was supposed to guarantee.

A popup, on top of a popup

The pattern that showed up most, across both sessions, had nothing to do with a single button. Every time a reviewer tried to cross-check a submitted document against what had been typed into the form, they'd click to open it — and a second popup would appear on top of the first, burying the exact thing they needed to compare it against. They'd close one, glance at the form, reopen the document, and repeat, sometimes three or four times per section. Nobody mentioned it out loud; it had just become part of how the screen worked. I only caught it because I was counting.

The fix was to stop stacking popups: the document now renders in a fixed left pane, with the review form staying visible on the right. CX Portal already had a split-panel layout documented in the system for a different screen — I didn't design a new pattern, I applied an existing one to a problem it happened to solve.

The gap neither session caught

One issue didn't show up in either recording at all. A reviewer mentioned it in passing after the second session had already wrapped: some clients send their documents straight to a broker, who handles onboarding on their behalf, and she'd had no way to add a document for them — the flow only ever assumed it came from the client directly. It wasn't dramatic, but once I checked, it was a real gap rather than an edge case.

I added an "Add New" action to the documents table, built from the same table and button components already in the system, so an employee can upload on a client's behalf using the exact same flow as everything else.

The one I found watching myself back

The last one wasn't in my notes from either session — I noticed it a few days later, reviewing my own recordings. The client's identity, AML score, compliance tier, and the Approve/Reject actions all lived in a header that scrolled away with the rest of the page, so checking a section near the bottom of a long application against the risk context at the top meant scrolling all the way back up, every time.

It was the simplest fix of the five: pin the existing header component in place instead of redesigning it. Risk context and the decision itself stay reachable through the entire review, no matter how far down the page a reviewer is.

02 — Impact

What five small fixes added up to

None of these five changes touched CX Portal's visual language, and none of them needed a new pattern for anyone to learn — every one reused a piece of the system I'd already helped build. Individually small, together they removed a steady amount of friction from a workflow the compliance team ran through dozens of times a day. These are directional estimates from flow analysis — a mix of measured and unmeasured usage, not a controlled study, stated plainly rather than dressed up as precise.

Fix Productivity impact  
Comment log Removes roughly one out-of-system message (Slack or email) per reassigned application  
Edit + re-review Turns a previously unsupported error state into a two-click product fix, no manager escalation needed  
Split document view Cuts roughly three open/close popup cycles per section down to a single persistent view  
Employee upload Replaces a problem that used to need backend support with a simple employee upload  
Static header Removes a scroll-to-top round trip every time risk context or the approve/reject action is needed  
03 — Reflection

What I'd do differently with more room

Watching a screenshare found problems a heuristic pass wouldn't have. None of the habits I logged would have shown up on a checklist review — they only became visible by watching two people work through real applications and noticing where they slowed down.

Some of these problems could have been solved more completely by rethinking the entire flow. But this was an already-built product with a real timeline behind it — the brief was to fix what was actively costing the team time, not to redesign around it, and co-owning the design system made that constraint easier to work inside rather than around.

Contrast validation came after the fact, not before it. WCAG 2.2 ratios are documented in the color system now, but checking against them should have been part of choosing the palette originally, not a pass I ran afterward.

Design tokens weren't in place yet. The color and type documentation exists as a reference, but without tokens, keeping implementation in sync with it still depends on someone checking by hand.

Appendix — the toolkit I was already working with

Every fix above reused a piece of a system I'd co-built

By the time I ran these sessions, my co-designer and I had already spent months building CX Portal's design system alongside every feature we shipped — colors, type, a 12-column grid, and a component library covering navigation, buttons, tables, search, and icons. That history is why none of the fixes above needed a blank page: each one just reached further into a kit that already existed.

Colors

Primary, secondary, and semantic color styles

We built a purposeful set of neutral, brand, and semantic colors as the starting point for every screen, and added WCAG 2.2 contrast ratios to the system itself so accessibility was something we could design against, not just check for afterward.

Typography

A single typeface, scaled and weighted for hierarchy

We picked Roboto for legibility and its familiarity on SaaS products, then built out sizes from 12 to 34px across Display, Header, Title, Headline, Body, and Caption styles, each in four weights — Regular, Medium, Semibold, and Bold — so hierarchy and prioritization stayed under control without a second typeface to manage.

Grids & spaces

A 12-column grid with an 8px spacing rhythm

The layout grid excludes the side nav, runs 32px outer margins with 24px gutters, and keeps every screen visually consistent against every other one. Spacing throughout is built from multiples of 8px, with 4px used in a handful of tighter spots like icon-to-label gaps.

Side nav

One component, three states

The side nav appears on every screen in the product, so we built it once as a single component with inactive, active, and notification states, instead of letting each page invent its own version.

CTA / buttons

Sized and stated for every use case

The button component covers three sizes — L, M, and S — with variants for state, type, and icon position, so every Approve, Reject, and secondary action across the product pulls from the same set instead of a one-off style built for that screen alone.

Tables

Zebra-striped, since most of the product is data

Most CX Portal screens show data as tables, so this became one of the most heavily used components in the whole system. Rows alternate color to help a reviewer scan a long table and keep their place across dozens of applications without losing their row.

Search, filter & pagination

Built to scale with the number of filters a table needs

The search bar supports a variable number of filters depending on the table it sits above, and pagination exists as separate variants for the two table sizes in the product, so a reviewer can jump to a specific page instead of only stepping forward and back one at a time.

Icons

Consistent size and line weight, no filled icons

Every icon sits in a 24x24px frame at a 1.5px line weight. We dropped filled icons entirely — the rest of the product was already visually heavy with tables and data, so outlined icons kept the interface from feeling more crowded than it needed to. Active and inactive states are told apart by color and background instead of icon style.
