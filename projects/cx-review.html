CLEARISK · CX PORTAL — ONBOARDING REVIEW

Fixing four friction points in a live client-review workflow

I co-built CX Portal's design system as one of two designers on the product. A few months after launch, I sat with the compliance team reviewing client applications and watched their review screen quietly work against them — so I diagnosed where it broke down and shipped four fixes inside the system we'd already built, without touching its visual language.

My contribution Design system (co-creator) · Workflow observation · Flow diagnosis · Interaction design · UI design
Project overview

A trade-processing platform built to replace a patchwork of tools

CleaRisk is a derivatives trading platform offering trade processing and client lifecycle management for financial institutions. The team had been running onboarding, back-office work, documents, and agreements across several disconnected tools, so they set out to build one piece of software — CX Portal — to hold client lifecycle management end to end.

I joined as one of two UX/UI designers on CX Portal, and spent my first stretch on the product co-building its design system alongside every new screen we shipped: colors, type, a 12-column grid, and a component library covering navigation, buttons, tables, search, and icons. Once the core product was live, my role shifted from designing new screens to going back through the ones already shipped and fixing what wasn't working — this case study is one of those passes.

01 — How I diagnosed it

I watched the review team work before I touched a single screen

The review screen wasn't broken in any obvious way — nothing crashed, nothing was mislabeled, nothing failed a basic heuristic check. So instead of starting from a checklist, I asked to sit in on a screenshare with two of the compliance reviewers who used the screen every day, and watched them work through a stack of real applications from start to finish.

I wasn't there to ask for opinions about the screen. I was watching for the moments where someone paused, re-read a field twice, tabbed out to Slack or email mid-review, or turned to a teammate to ask a question out loud. Every one of those moments got logged with a rough timestamp and a plain description of what had just happened — not a diagnosis yet, just a record of exactly where the flow made someone stop. I ran the same watch-and-log pass twice, a week apart, with a different reviewer each time, so I wasn't reading too much into one person's habits or one unusually messy application.

02 — What surfaced

Three habits were quietly costing the review team time

Across both sessions, the pauses clustered around three moments. None of them were dramatic — nobody was stuck for more than a minute or two at a time — but they repeated on almost every application, which is what made them worth fixing before anything else.

01
Handoff problem

No reasoning for rejection or approval

Approve and Reject were plain buttons with nothing attached to them. Partway through the second session, a reviewer picked up an application her colleague had rejected that same morning, read through it once, and asked the question that stuck with me for the rest of the audit:

"Wait — why was this rejected?"

Applications get reassigned between employees on the same role constantly, and without a logged reason, that question had nowhere to go except back to whoever made the original call — if they were even still online to ask.

02
Recovery problem

No correction after approval

Later in the same session, a reviewer caught a typo in a field she'd already approved and went looking for a way to fix it. There wasn't one — once a section was approved, the button changed to "Approved," the client-facing state locked in, and Reject disabled right alongside it.

"How do I edit any of these details in case of a mistake?"

There wasn't an answer in the product. She flagged it to a manager instead, which is exactly the kind of workaround that shouldn't need to exist for something this small.

03
Cross-checking problem

The document viewer blocked the form

The third pattern showed up every time someone tried to cross-check a submitted document against what had been typed into the form. Clicking the document opened a popup on top of the review popup, so the two things a reviewer actually needed side by side were never on screen together — they'd close one, glance at the form, reopen the document, and repeat, sometimes three or four times per section.

03 — The system I'd already helped build

Every fix had to work inside a kit I'd co-created

By the time I ran these sessions, my co-designer and I had already spent months building CX Portal's design system alongside every feature we shipped — colors, type, a 12-column grid, and a component library covering navigation, buttons, tables, search, and icons. That history mattered for this pass: fixing the review screen was never a blank-page exercise. Every change further down this page reuses a piece of that system rather than introducing something new, which is also part of why none of it needed a new pattern for anyone else to learn.

Colors

Primary, secondary, and semantic color styles

We built a purposeful set of neutral, brand, and semantic colors as the starting point for every screen, and added WCAG 2.2 contrast ratios to the system itself so accessibility was something we could design against, not just check for afterward.

Typography

A single typeface, scaled and weighted for hierarchy

We picked Roboto for legibility and its familiarity on SaaS products, then built out sizes from 12 to 34px across Display, Header, Title, Headline, Body, and Caption styles, each in four weights — Regular, Medium, Semibold, and Bold — so hierarchy and prioritization stayed under control without a second typeface to manage.

Grids & spaces

A 12-column grid with an 8px spacing rhythm

The layout grid excludes the side nav, runs 32px outer margins with 24px gutters, and keeps every screen visually consistent against every other one. Spacing throughout is built from multiples of 8px, with 4px used in a handful of tighter spots like icon-to-label gaps.

Side nav

One component, three states

The side nav appears on every screen in the product, so we built it once as a single component with inactive, active, and notification states, instead of letting each page invent its own version.

CTA / buttons

Sized and stated for every use case

The button component covers three sizes — L, M, and S — with variants for state, type, and icon position, so every Approve, Reject, and secondary action across the product pulls from the same set instead of a one-off style built for that screen alone.

Tables

Zebra-striped, since most of the product is data

Most CX Portal screens show data as tables, so this became one of the most heavily used components in the whole system. Rows alternate color to help a reviewer scan a long table and keep their place across dozens of applications without losing their row.

Search, filter & pagination

Built to scale with the number of filters a table needs

The search bar supports a variable number of filters depending on the table it sits above, and pagination exists as separate variants for the two table sizes in the product, so a reviewer can jump to a specific page instead of only stepping forward and back one at a time.

Icons

Consistent size and line weight, no filled icons

Every icon sits in a 24x24px frame at a 1.5px line weight. We dropped filled icons entirely — the rest of the product was already visually heavy with tables and data, so outlined icons kept the interface from feeling more crowded than it needed to. Active and inactive states are told apart by color and background instead of icon style.

04 — Four fixes, and one I couldn't leave alone

Small changes that reused a system I already knew

Every fix below traces back to one of the three moments from the screenshare sessions, plus one more I flagged myself once I saw how much time it was quietly costing on every single review. None of these touch CX Portal's visual language — the system was already right. They just needed to reach further into this one screen than it currently did.

1 / Comment required on Approve or Reject

Surfaced in the Timeline, with a date and time, so any handoff carries its reasoning

I'd already co-built the Timeline component on this exact screen, originally just to log system events — submissions, edits, third-party checks passing or failing. Extending it to also carry human decisions meant reaching for a piece that already existed instead of inventing a new one. Approve and Reject now require a comment before either will fire, and that comment posts to the Timeline exactly the way a system event does: a timestamp, the reviewer's name, and the reasoning itself, all in one place.

The next person to open the case sees that reasoning before they see anything else on the screen — no Slack thread to dig through, no waiting on whoever made the original call to log back on.

System reused:Timeline component, extended to log human decisions alongside system events
Removes:roughly one out-of-system Slack or email message per assignment, where time was being wasted on ambiguity

2 / Edit re-opens an approved section for re-review

Needed a way to correct an approved section without quietly breaking the audit trail

Rather than design a new "unlock" interaction from scratch, I extended the Edit control already used everywhere else in the form. Clicking it unlocks the fields and logs every change to the Timeline the same way any other edit does — but the part that actually mattered was what happens on Save: the section drops back to Pending instead of staying marked Approved.

That one state change was the real fix. A silent edit to an already-approved section would have solved the reviewer's immediate problem while quietly breaking the thing the approval was supposed to guarantee. Moving it back to Pending means the next reviewer sees it needs a second look, instead of trusting an approval that's no longer accurate.

System reused:The same Edit control and Timeline logging used on every other field in the form
Removes:a previously unsupported error state, now a two-click product fix instead of a manager escalation

3 / Document viewer moved side-by-side

From a popup over a popup to a split panel where document and form are visible at once

The fix itself is simple to describe: instead of stacking a second popup on top of the first, the document now renders in a fixed left pane, and the review form stays visible on the right. What made it fast to build was that CX Portal already had a split-panel layout documented in the system for a different screen — I didn't design a new interaction pattern, I applied an existing one to a problem it happened to solve.

The document and the form a reviewer needs to compare are on screen together now, for the entire time they're checking one against the other.

System reused:The split-panel layout already documented elsewhere in CX Portal
Removes:roughly three open/close popup cycles per section, down to a single persistent view

4 / Adding documents on a client's behalf

A smaller fix, for a case the original flow never accounted for

Documents were assumed to always come from the client directly. In practice, some clients send files straight to their broker, who fills out onboarding on their behalf — and the employee handling that case had no way to add a document for them. It didn't come up in the screenshare sessions themselves, but a reviewer mentioned it in passing afterward, and once I checked, it was a real gap rather than an edge case.

I added an "Add New" action to the documents section, built from the same table and button components already in the system, so employees can add documents on a client's behalf using the exact same table and the exact same review flow as everything else.

System reused:Existing table component and the primary button, same size and state as elsewhere
Removes:a problem that used to need backend support, down to a simple employee upload
Flagged, not assigned

★ Client header stays fixed while the body scrolls

Originally scrolled away with the page — I changed it to stay static

This one wasn't in my notes from either session — I noticed it myself while reviewing my own recordings afterward. The client's identity, AML score, compliance tier, and the Approve/Reject actions all lived in a header that scrolled away with the rest of the page. Checking a section near the bottom of a long application against the risk context at the top meant scrolling all the way back up, every time.

It was the simplest fix of the five: pin the existing header component in place rather than redesign it. Risk context and the decision itself stay reachable through the entire review, no matter how far down the page a reviewer is.

System reused:The existing client header component, given a fixed position instead of a new layout
Removes:a scroll-to-top round trip, every time risk context or the approve/reject action is needed

06 — Reflection

What I'd do differently with more room

Watching a screenshare found problems a heuristic pass wouldn't have. None of the three habits I logged would have shown up on a checklist review — they only became visible by watching two people work through real applications and noticing where they slowed down.

Some of these problems could have been solved more completely by rethinking the entire flow. But this was an already-built product with a real timeline behind it — the brief was to fix what was actively causing problems, not to redesign around them, and co-owning the design system made that constraint easier to work inside rather than around.

Contrast validation came after the fact, not before it. WCAG 2.2 ratios are documented in the color system now, but checking against them should have been part of choosing the palette originally, not a pass I ran afterward.

Design tokens weren't in place yet. The color and type documentation exists as a reference, but without tokens, keeping implementation in sync with it still depends on someone checking by hand.
