CLEARISK · CX PORTAL — ONBOARDING REVIEW · B2B SAAS

Improving productivity on a live onboarding review workflow (B2B SaaS)

I co-built CX Portal's design system as one of two designers on the product. Months after launch, I sat in on two live reviewer sessions to see how the screen actually held up under daily use — and found five small frictions quietly costing the team time on every application. Here's what I found, and why each fix works.

My contribution Design system (co-creator) · Workflow observation · Flow diagnosis · Interaction design · UI design
Project overview

A trade-processing platform built to replace a patchwork of tools

CleaRisk is a derivatives trading platform offering trade processing and client lifecycle management for financial institutions. CX Portal was built to bring onboarding, back-office work, documents, and agreements — previously spread across several disconnected tools — into one product.

I joined as one of two UX/UI designers on CX Portal and spent my first stretch co-building its design system alongside every screen we shipped. Once the product was live, my role shifted from designing new screens to watching how the ones already shipped held up under real, daily use. This case study is about the morning that shift mattered most.

Scope

This screen was already built and shipped. The brief wasn't "redesign it" — it was to reduce friction and improve productivity for the review team within the existing system, and add only what was absolutely required. I'm under NDA on this product: everything shown here is rebuilt with placeholder data, real structure and logic, no real client or company information.

01 — Two mornings with the review queue

I watched two reviewers work through real applications, start to finish

Nothing on the screen was broken in a way a heuristic checklist would catch. So instead of an audit, I sat in on a screenshare with two compliance reviewers and watched them move through a stack of real applications, twice, a week apart, with a different reviewer each time — so I wasn't reading too much into one person's habits.

"Wait — why was this rejected?"

Twenty minutes in, a reviewer picked up an application her colleague had rejected that morning, read it once, then asked:

"Wait — why was this rejected?"

Nothing was attached to the Reject button to answer her. This is a review queue, not a single reviewer's inbox — applications get reassigned constantly, so a decision with no reasoning attached doesn't just cost the person who made it, it costs whoever inherits it next. I extended the Timeline component I'd already built on this screen — until now it only logged system events — so Approve and Reject require a comment before they'll fire, and that comment lands in the Timeline with a timestamp and the reviewer's name. The next person opens the case and sees the reasoning first, not last.

"How do I edit any of these details in case of a mistake?"

An hour later, the same reviewer caught a typo in a field she'd already approved and had no way to fix it — once approved, the section locked, and Reject disabled with it.

"How do I edit any of these details in case of a mistake?"

She flagged it to a manager — a workaround that shouldn't exist for something this small. The harder part of this fix wasn't unlocking the field, it was what happens after: I made saving an edit drop the section back to Pending instead of leaving it marked Approved. An approval is a claim that someone checked the section and it was correct — letting an edit happen silently underneath an "Approved" label would have solved her problem while quietly making that claim false for every application it touched.

A popup, on top of a popup

The pattern that showed up most, across both sessions, wasn't one moment — it was a rhythm. Cross-checking a submitted document against the form meant opening a popup on top of the review popup, so the two things a reviewer needed side by side were never on screen together. Close one, glance at the form, reopen the document, repeat — three or four times per section, often enough that neither reviewer mentioned it out loud. It had just become the cost of doing the job.

I moved the document into a fixed left pane with the form staying visible on the right, using a split-panel layout already documented elsewhere in the system. The underlying task — compare two pieces of information — never needs two things to take turns on screen; it needs them both there at once.

The gap neither session caught

A reviewer mentioned this one after the second session had wrapped: some clients send documents straight to a broker handling onboarding on their behalf, and she had no way to add a document for them — the flow only ever assumed it came from the client directly. I added an "Add New" action to the documents table, reusing the same table and button components already in the system, so an employee can upload on a client's behalf without a special-case flow.

The one I found watching myself back

The last one wasn't in either session's notes — I caught it days later, reviewing my own recordings. The client's identity, AML score, compliance tier, and the Approve/Reject actions lived in a header that scrolled away with the page, so checking a late section against the risk context at the top meant scrolling all the way back up, every time. The information that matters most for a decision shouldn't be the thing that's hardest to keep in view while you're making it. I pinned the existing header in place — the simplest fix of the five, and arguably the one with the clearest logic behind it.

02 — Impact

What five small fixes added up to

None of these changes touched CX Portal's visual language, and none needed a new pattern for anyone to learn — each one reused a piece of a system I'd already helped build. Individually small, together they removed a steady amount of friction from a workflow the compliance team ran through dozens of times a day. These are directional estimates from flow analysis, not a controlled study, stated plainly rather than dressed up as precise.

Fix Productivity impact  
Comment log Removes roughly one out-of-system message (Slack or email) per reassigned application  
Edit + re-review Turns an unsupported error state into a two-click fix, no manager escalation needed  
Split document view Cuts roughly three open/close popup cycles per section down to a single persistent view  
Employee upload Replaces a problem that used to need backend support with a simple employee upload  
Static header Removes a scroll-to-top round trip every time risk context is needed  
03 — Reflection

What I'd do differently with more room

Watching a screenshare found problems a heuristic pass wouldn't have. None of the habits I logged would have shown up on a checklist review — they only became visible by watching two people work through real applications and noticing where they slowed down.

This wasn't a redesign, and that constraint shaped every decision. The brief was to fix what was actively costing the team time, not rethink the flow — co-owning the design system made that constraint something to work inside rather than around.

Contrast validation came after the fact, not before it. WCAG 2.2 ratios are documented in the color system now, but checking against them should have been part of choosing the palette originally.
