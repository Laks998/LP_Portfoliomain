CLEARISK · CX DASHBOARD · B2B SAAS

Bringing the CX team's daily work into one place

Every working day for the CX team started the same way: emails, Slack, calls, then checking pipeline, pending, in-review and rejected applications across different parts of the CX Portal. I designed a role-based CX Dashboard from the ground up so the team had one place to see their tasks for the day, how much work was left, what needed action and who was available to work with.

My contribution Research · User interviews · Workflow analysis · Qualitative synthesis · Information architecture · User flows · Wireframing · Interaction design · UI design · Prototyping
Project overview

About Clearisk

Clearisk is a B2B platform supporting financial institutions and their operational workflows. The CX Portal was used to support customer onboarding and the work involved in reviewing and progressing applications.

As the product and operational workflows grew, there was an opportunity to give the CX team a dedicated place to understand and manage their work.

02 / The problem

The work was in the product.
The workflow wasn't.

The CX Portal held the applications, but not the day around them. When the team started work, they first went through emails, Slack and calls, then checked pipeline, pending, in-review and rejected applications in different parts of the portal.

There was no single place to see what their tasks were for the day, how much was left, what needed action, or who they needed to work with to move an application forward.

The work lived in the product. Knowing where to start did not.

A typical start to the day

The problem was not that information was missing.

It was spread across tools and screens, so the team had to piece their day together before they could start on it.

03 / Understanding the work

Before designing the dashboard, I needed to understand how the CX team actually worked.

Rather than starting with a collection of dashboard widgets, I first mapped the existing workflow. The goal was to understand:

  • What happens when an application enters the workflow?
  • What information does the CX team need at each stage?
  • Where does work move outside the product?
  • What requires follow-up?
  • How do users know what needs attention?
  • What information do they need before taking action?

Where the workflow breaks

The product was only one part of the workflow.

Understanding the surrounding coordination became important to understanding what the dashboard needed to solve.

05 / Research

I started with the people doing the work.

The first stage was understanding the CX team's day-to-day work and the information they relied on to manage applications.

I used research and interviews to understand how users currently navigated their work, what information they checked repeatedly and where coordination happened outside the product.

06 / Interviews

10 / Structure

Turning research into structure

The dashboard needed to make the user's work easier to understand before asking them to navigate deeper into the product.

I used the research findings to define the information architecture and decide what belonged at the dashboard level versus inside an individual application.

The dashboard needed to answer three questions

01What needs my attention?
02Where does my work stand?
03What should I do next?

These questions became the basis for the dashboard hierarchy.

  • How should users move from overview information into actual work?

14 / Solution

A dashboard built around the work that needs attention

The final dashboard brings the most relevant information into one place, allowing CX users to understand their workload before opening individual applications.

01

How much work is left

A single progress bar shows how many of today's tasks are done and how many are left. Below it, every stage the team used to check separately (pipeline, pending, in review, rejected) sits in one row, next to what has been completed today.

02

Actions and new tasks

The full onboarding table had seven columns. On the dashboard, each row answers three questions instead: whose application is it, where is it in Onboarding, Compliance and Manager review, and who is it waiting on right now. New tasks, resubmissions and work that needs cover are tagged, and each row leads straight into the review.

03

Seeing work get done

The activity feed records what has been completed, approved, assigned and reassigned through the day. Finished work gets a clear tick instead of quietly leaving the queue.

04

Who's in, who's out

Everyone who is part of the user's workflow, and whether they are present or on leave. If someone in a role is off, the user knows straight away who needs to pick up their applications.

15 / Interaction

The dashboard is only useful if it leads somewhere.

The goal was not to create another page for users to look at.

The dashboard needed to create a clear path into the actual work.

Overview Context Action

The interaction moves from overview to context to action, rather than requiring users to reconstruct what needs attention before entering the application.

17 / Outcome

From fragmented coordination to a clearer starting point

The dashboard did not replace the CX Portal, email or Slack.

It gave the team one place to start the day and come back to: what's assigned, what's left, what needs action and who's around to help.

19 / Reflection

What I learned

A dashboard is not a collection of widgets.

The most useful question was not "What information can we show?"

It was: "What does the user need to understand before they can decide what to do?"

The surrounding workflow matters.

Looking only at the CX Portal would have missed part of the problem.

The work also happened around the product through communication, follow-ups and coordination.

Structure comes before polish.

The most important design decisions happened before the final UI: understanding the workflow, synthesising the research, defining the information architecture and deciding what deserved priority.

People are part of the workflow.

An application can stall because the person who needs to review it is away, not because of anything on screen.

Showing who is present or on leave turned out to be as important as showing the applications themselves.

The goal was not more information.

The goal was to make the information the team already had easier to understand and act on.
